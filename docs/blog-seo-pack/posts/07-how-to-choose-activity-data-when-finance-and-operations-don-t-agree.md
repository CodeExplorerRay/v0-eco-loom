# How to Choose Activity Data When Finance and Operations Don’t Agree

Activity Data | April 1, 2026 | 4 min read

**Topic:** Activity Data
**Primary Keyword:** activity data
**Publish Date:** April 1, 2026
**Status:** Ready to Publish

This happens more often than people admit. Finance has one number. Operations has another. Both sound plausible. Both come from real systems. And now the sustainability team has to decide which one becomes part of the emissions record.

There is no magic formula for this. But there is a practical way to think about it.

## Start with what the number is meant to represent

Not every business number is built for the same purpose. Finance numbers are usually built for cost control, accrual logic, and accounting close. Operational numbers are often built for usage, throughput, or physical activity.

Carbon accounting needs activity data that reflects what actually happened in the real world. Sometimes finance has that. Sometimes operations does. Sometimes neither does cleanly.

The question is not “which team is right?” It is “which data stream better represents the physical activity that drives emissions?”

That framing changes the conversation immediately.

## Spend is not the same as activity

One common source of disagreement is when finance has spend data and operations has usage data. For example, finance may show total fuel cost, while operations tracks liters or gallons. Finance may have the travel expense total, while the booking tool has flight distance or trip detail.

If the goal is emissions calculation, usage usually wins when it is reliable enough. Spend can still be useful, especially for rough Scope 3 estimation, but it should not automatically override a better physical activity measure.

The trap is that finance data often looks cleaner because it is already reconciled. Clean does not always mean better for carbon accounting.

## Reconcile once, then write down the rule

A smart team does not relitigate the same disagreement every month. It reconciles the conflict once, documents the logic, and applies that rule consistently unless something material changes.

For example:

- use operational fuel volume for Scope 1 fleet reporting
- use finance-confirmed spend only when no reliable usage data exists
- prefer site utility consumption over landlord cost allocation when both are available

What matters is not just the decision. It is the repeatability of the decision.

## Real-world example: freight data

Imagine a company with regular third-party shipping. Finance can tell you total freight spend by month. Operations can give you partial lane data, but only for the largest carriers. Which source should be used?

In practice, the answer may be mixed. Use operational activity data where carrier or lane detail is available. Use a documented estimation method for the remainder. Then clearly mark that the category contains both measured and estimated components.

That is more useful than forcing everything into one system just because it feels cleaner.

## Disagreement is usually a systems issue, not a people issue

When finance and operations disagree, it is tempting to treat it like a coordination problem. Sometimes it is. But often the deeper issue is that the company has no shared rule for translating business data into emissions-ready activity data.

Once that rule exists, the conversations get easier. Not because the data becomes perfect, but because the purpose of the number becomes clearer.

People stop arguing about which spreadsheet “wins” and start talking about which source best captures the activity being reported.

That’s a much healthier place to be.

## Choose the source that can survive review

There is another useful test here: which number can stand up to review with the least amount of hand-waving? Sometimes finance has the cleaner audit trail. Sometimes operations has the better physical signal. Sometimes the answer is a documented blend, where one source provides the activity basis and another provides the completeness check.

The wrong move is choosing the number that feels politically easiest in the moment. That may smooth one meeting, but it usually creates more confusion later when somebody asks where the figure came from and why another internal source says something different.

When teams know they will have to defend the number later, the decision gets sharper. They stop asking which function “owns” the truth and start asking which source, method, and explanation are strong enough to be used repeatedly without inventing a fresh rationale every month.

That shift matters because good reporting decisions are not only about accuracy. They are also about durability. A method the team can explain consistently is worth more than a slightly better-looking number that falls apart under basic follow-up questions.

That is usually the difference between a one-time compromise and a reporting rule the business can keep living with.

And once teams experience that difference, the internal argument tends to become much less emotional.

**Key Takeaway:** When finance and operations disagree, choose the data that best reflects the underlying physical activity, then document the rule so the same argument does not repeat every month.
