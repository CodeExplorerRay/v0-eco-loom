# What Founders Get Wrong About Early ESG Work

Early ESG Strategy | October 17, 2026 | 4 min read

**Topic:** Early ESG Strategy
**Primary Keyword:** early ESG work
**Publish Date:** October 17, 2026
**Status:** Ready to Publish

Founders are used to moving fast, making imperfect decisions, and fixing things in motion. That mindset can be a strength in ESG work. It can also create avoidable mess if the company treats sustainability like a pitch topic before it treats it like an operating process.

Most early ESG mistakes are not malicious. They come from speed, optimism, and a tendency to underestimate how much reporting quality depends on boring internal discipline.

## They often think the first job is the public story

A lot of founders start with the external question: what should we say? Customers are asking. Investors are asking. Team members want clarity. So the company jumps toward a sustainability position before it has built a stable reporting spine.

That usually feels efficient at first. It becomes risky later when simple follow-up questions expose weak ownership, unclear methodology, or unsupported claims.

The better first question is not “what do we say?” It is “what can we actually support today?”

## They underestimate cross-functional work

Founders sometimes assume ESG can sit neatly inside one person or one team. In practice, even an early-stage program touches finance, operations, procurement, facilities, people, and often legal.

That does not mean everyone needs to become an expert. It does mean the company needs to accept that the work crosses boundaries.

When that reality is ignored, sustainability leads get stuck chasing inputs from functions that were never told they were part of the reporting system.

## Real-world example: the net-zero shortcut

A founder hears peers talking about net-zero commitments and wants the company to keep up. The team starts exploring claims before it has a reliable view of direct emissions, supplier visibility, or a stable review process. Six months later, the company is explaining ambition more clearly than it can explain the current inventory.

That does not make the ambition bad. It means the sequencing was off.

## Early ESG work often fails because the owner has no leverage

This is a quieter problem, but it matters a lot. A founder may assign sustainability work to a smart generalist or operations lead and assume that is enough. Then the same person spends half their time chasing utility files, asking procurement for supplier details, and trying to decode travel data they do not actually control.

That is not a talent problem. It is an authority problem. If the owner of ESG work has responsibility without leverage, the program slows down and the company starts blaming the topic instead of the operating setup.

## The best founder move is usually to narrow the first year

Founders do not need to be passive here. One of the most useful choices they can make is to narrow the first year of ESG work to something the company can actually support.

Maybe that means building a usable baseline and a monthly review cadence before promising bigger targets. Maybe it means focusing on a few material categories and saying less publicly for a while. That kind of restraint is rarely exciting, but it is often what keeps the work from collapsing under its own ambition.

## Founders often need fewer commitments and better control

The strongest early ESG posture is not always the boldest. Often it is the one with the clearest scope.

That may mean:

- a smaller number of supportable claims
- a disciplined first emissions baseline
- a tighter review process
- a real owner for the work

These do not create dramatic headlines. They create trust.

## The real founder job is resourcing and prioritization

A founder does not need to become the company’s carbon accountant. But they do need to decide whether ESG is being treated as a side request or as a real operating responsibility.

That decision shows up in resourcing, attention, and how seriously the company takes evidence quality when the answer is inconvenient.

## The market usually rewards honesty more than borrowed ambition

Founders sometimes worry that a cautious ESG posture will make the company look behind. In reality, sophisticated buyers and partners can usually tell the difference between a company that is being careful and one that is borrowing maturity it has not built yet.

A founder who says, “Here is what we have measured, here is what we are still building, and here is how we review the data,” often sounds more credible than one who jumps to a sweeping commitment without the operating foundation to support it. That is not glamorous advice. But it is usually the one that causes less damage later.

And for early-stage teams, credibility tends to compound. Once people trust the company’s reporting discipline, it becomes much easier to expand the work later without sounding like the story changed again.

**Key Takeaway:** Early ESG work usually fails when founders treat the public story as the first milestone instead of building a supportable internal process first.
